# Adversarial review log

The pipeline was reviewed in rounds by a reviewer playing a skeptical senior data engineer on an AWS Glue/Athena team, who was looking for reasons *not* to hire the author. The reviewer was an AI agent given that role (see [How this was built](../README.md#how-this-was-built)), not a person. In each round it worked from a clean checkout with no access to the author's notes, ran everything, and hand-built hostile inputs, with a script for every finding it reported. Each finding is recorded with its verdict and what changed. Every fix that changed behaviour has a regression test.

## Round 1

**Reviewer's verdict:** lean no. The biggest reason was that three headline claims ("incremental equals full", "quarantine instead of drop", reconciliation catching mis-paid receipts) broke on small hand-made inputs outside the generator's defect catalog, and that the 100% scorecard largely graded the pipeline against its own generator.

## Round 3

**Reviewer's verdict:** do not advance. The biggest reason was that the reconciliation engine had become the riskiest part of the system: its rules for when quarantine "explains" a variance were hard to reason about, one quarantined record could explain two rows at once, and an unreferenced bank debit or a jump in a `BALANCE FORWARD` line was not flagged by any check. The reviewer also showed incremental and full runs disagreeing, and two ways to lose bronze data.

## Round 4

**Reviewer's verdict:** lean no. The biggest reason was that the round-3 fixes closed the exact repro each finding came with, not the rule behind it: the unflagged large debit came back once it was labelled `BALANCE FORWARD`, the watermark fix could be bypassed two more ways, and the `cp -p` ordering fix was applied in one of the two places that rank file versions. Six of the reviewer's seven mutations against round-3 rules survived the suite. What held up: incremental runs over 30 uneven, mid-folder delivery chunks matched a full run on every table; metadata recovery survived log loss, a crash before logging, and a revert after log loss; the reconciliation redesign gave the reviewer no way to clear a real variance.

## Round 5

**Reviewer's verdict:** lean no. The biggest reason was the same as round 4: fixes closed the exact repro rather than the rule. A touched file that was rejected for its *content* still cleared exit 3; a stale re-send in different bytes was not raised; a self-consistent file in a future folder moved the watermark; and a 250,000 debit dated 2035 sat "open" instead of becoming an exception. This round's fixes were therefore written as rules, each with a test aimed at a variation rather than the reviewer's repro. What held up: Glue parity on every hostile value the reviewer tried (invalid dates, second 60, NUL, U+2028, full-width digits, Unicode trim across all code points); no way to make a net cash outflow match; crash recovery and log repair as documented.

## Where the loop stopped

The plan was about five rounds, or fewer if only nits remained. Five rounds ran. Rounds 1–3 found design problems (reconciliation that could hide errors, ordering that made incremental runs differ from full ones, recovery that could delete data), and round 3 replaced the reconciliation engine. Rounds 4–5 found that fixes had been written against each repro rather than the rule behind it. Round 5's fixes are stated as rules with boundary tests, and every round-5 fix has a test that fails on the round-4 code. A further round would most likely find more edges of the same kind; what is still known to be missing is listed under *Assumptions and limitations* in the README and *If this is picked up again* in the design notes.
